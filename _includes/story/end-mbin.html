The terminal was nearly empty. Cleaners worked under the fluorescent lights. He lay across a row of joined seats with the bag under his head and waited to board.

Around three he worked it out again. If he had stayed off the grass, he would have caught the ferry and the flight. He would have spent the night at his mother’s and gone to the corner with her at first light. Instead he was here, and she would stand there alone.

He remembered the white pages beneath the new leaves and the hands that had held them. He would never learn what happened at the gate afterward. He would still have crossed the grass.

At a quarter to four he went to the gate. His seat was over the wing.

They landed a little after six. Daylight had reached the hangars.

A taxi took the shore road into the city. At the corner stood a plane tree and a shuttered grocery. Where the block had been, the barrier was folded back. Behind it lay a heap of damp rubble.

It was over.

His mother stood alone before the rubble, hands behind her back, in the place she had kept to the end.

She did not turn at his step behind her. She was looking at something on the ground.

He looked at her, then followed her gaze.

A photograph lay face up. In it, a man stood on a step with a child beside him at knee height. Behind them, the doorframe bore its first pencil line.

The bag stayed on his shoulder.

There was dust on the new leaves.
