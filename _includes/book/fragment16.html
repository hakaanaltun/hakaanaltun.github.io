None of Us Are

We were standing outside Teacher’s Bar on a Saturday night. We had been sitting inside for hours, and stepping outside felt like waking up. Midnight was approaching and Kadife Sokak was packed, with people spilling off the sidewalks into the road. We could hear people laughing at the tables around us.

My back was to the street. I was talking to a friend. Whatever I was saying has disappeared, though the conviction has stayed.

Then two hands landed on my shoulders. They arrived with purpose, firm enough to enter the body and calm enough to make resistance feel absurd. By the time I understood that someone was standing behind me, he had already leaned close to my ear.

In a deep, resonant voice, he said, “You are not special. None of us are.”

Then he let go. Before I could turn properly, he was walking away. I watched him pass between groups of people and disappear behind shoulders into the movement of the street.

My friend was still talking beside me. The interruption straightened my shoulders and quickened my pulse. Electricity moved through me as I kept looking at the place where the man had disappeared.

I felt chosen.

There was a year when I became close with people who seemed to live slightly higher than the rest of us. When the bill came, no one counted. I stopped counting too.

One evening, my oldest friend joined us. He was the same person he had always been, and I had loved him for years. That night, I watched the others measure him. A glance lasted too long. Someone smiled with patience. Nothing was said, which made it worse.

I felt myself move away from him. For a while, I called it growth.

We were on the couch. Some show was on, and a couple on screen was fighting about something small. We watched them as you watch the street from behind a window. I felt Seda’s hand find mine on the cushion. Neither of us said anything. But the room had an agreement in it. We were not the kind who would shout. We were not the kind who would keep score. The silence between us felt like proof.

Three nights later she was standing by the kitchen counter. The kettle had just clicked off. I was at the stove, back turned.

“I’m trying to figure out how to tell you something without it sounding like I’m blaming you,” she said.

“Tell me what?”

She went quiet. “You don’t even know what it is.” She stopped. “I can’t believe you don’t even know.”

“You haven’t said anything yet.”

“I shouldn’t have to.”

I turned around. I was holding some papers. I wanted the papers to feel heavier than they were. I wanted the moment to need me more than it did.

“So say it.”

“I just tried.”

“You said you were trying to figure out how to say it. That’s not saying it.”

“And you just proved why I can’t.”

The fight lasted three minutes and never reached the subject. Neither of us raised a voice. Still, I am measuring the disappointment.

I stood there under the yellow light, and felt a phantom pressure on my shoulders, firm enough to enter the body.
