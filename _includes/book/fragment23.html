The Ease

The garden door was open and the breeze was moving the curtain toward the living room. Deniz had her phone propped against the flour tin because the tripod was in the storage room, under a pile of things she would have to move to get at it. She had already filmed four recipes with the phone against the tin and it seemed to work well enough.

The dog was sitting on her left with his tongue out, waiting for something to fall. She was stuffing squash blossoms, which took a while, and it was difficult to show what she was doing because her fingers covered most of the flower while she was putting the filling in.

Her husband came in from the hall with the trash bag in one hand and the car keys in the other.

“What’s this one?”

“Kabak çiçeği dolması.”

“I meant the phone.”

She wiped her hands. “The videos are for the book. There’ll be a QR code under each recipe so people can watch me make it.”

“Oh, nice,” he said. “I’m getting her at four.”

“They said rain later.” She turned back to the tray. “Drive carefully.”

“It’s ten minutes. It hasn’t rained since May.”

“I know.” She pressed the filling down with her thumb. “I hate driving in the rain.”

He leaned in, kissed her cheek somewhere near the ear, and took the bag out through the garden door.

The dog had not moved. She tore off a piece of the filling, the plain part without the mint, and held it down. It was gone before her hand had opened all the way.

Outside, the lid of the bin. A car door. The engine.

The light coming through the garden door had thinned.

She looked at the phone, which had been recording the whole time.

She decided to keep it.

· · ·

The market smelled of melon and wet stone. She had come for herbs and had already been talked into a kilo of tomatoes she did not need, which happened most weeks.

She was pressing her thumb against a bunch of dill when she looked up.

A man four or five stalls down, on the other side, half turned away. The shoulders were right. So was the way he stood while he waited.

For a second it was Şafak.

Then he turned and it wasn’t him. It was a man about that age, buying fish.

She bought the dill. She bought the tomatoes she did not need.

Her phone rang while she was putting the bags in the back.

“I can’t get her,” her husband said. “They’re calling me in. I have to go.”

“Alright.”

“I’m sorry.”

“Go.”

It had started while she was inside, not hard, but enough to make the road shine.

She sat in the car for a moment before she started it.

It was ten minutes. She took twenty.
