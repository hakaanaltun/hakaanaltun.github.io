Şafak

The receptionist had reading glasses pushed up into her hair and a pen behind each ear.

“Şafak?” she said.

The waiting room smelled like someone had recently microwaved fish and then tried to cover it up with lavender. Şafak had been in one of the orange plastic chairs for thirty-two minutes. A woman in a stiff blazer sat reading her phone with surgical focus. Next to her, a young man kept adjusting a short tie. Şafak counted the adjustments. He stopped at nine.

He stood up before the second syllable had landed.

She pointed down a corridor with the pen from her left ear. “Second door on the right.”

The office was larger than he expected. A wide mahogany desk, nearly empty—just a closed laptop, a glass of water with no coaster, and a single manila folder, very thin, positioned exactly in the center. Behind the desk, a window that looked out onto a construction site. A crane was moving something slowly across the sky, and for a moment Şafak watched it, trying to figure out what it was carrying.

The man behind the desk stood up to shake his hand. He was short and wide, built like a filing cabinet, with thick fingers and a watch that sat too loose on his wrist, sliding down toward his knuckles every time he gestured. His eyebrows were two gray caterpillars conducting an orchestra only they could hear. He wore a shirt that had been ironed recently and well, except for one crease along the left forearm that he kept smoothing with his other hand.

Şafak’s arm fell against the side of the chair. His own silver watch slid down his wrist.

“Sit, sit,” the man said, waving at a chair. The chair was leather, tall-backed. Şafak sat.

“So,” he said, opening the manila folder. He looked at whatever was inside for a long time. Şafak resisted the urge to lean forward and peek.

“Tell me about yourself.”

Şafak told him. University, internship at an agency, some freelance social media work, a certificate in Google Analytics that he’d earned on a Sunday afternoon while eating leftover börek. He didn’t mention the börek. The man nodded throughout, his eyebrows rising and falling with the rhythm of Şafak’s sentences.

“Good, good,” he said. He closed the folder. Opened it again. Closed it.

“And your English—how is it?”

“Fluent,” Şafak said. “I lived in England for a year.”

“Let’s continue in English, then.”

This made sense. International company, probably. Maslak was full of them. Şafak straightened in his chair.

“What do you think,” the man said, switching to English with an accent that was hard to place, “about the current state of the Turkish economy?”

Şafak blinked.

“It’s in a transitional period,” he said. “There’s inflation pressure, but the export sector is showing resilience, and there are structural reforms underway that could—”

The man wrote something in the folder. His pen moved quickly. Şafak tried to read it upside down. He couldn’t.

“Now. If you had to choose between—let me think how to phrase this—between institutional loyalty and operational efficiency, and you could only preserve one, which would you eliminate?”

“I’d argue they’re not mutually exclusive,” Şafak said. “But if I had to choose, I’d preserve efficiency, because loyalty without results eventually erodes itself.”

The man did not answer. He picked up the glass of water, took a sip, and put it back down in a different spot. Then he smiled, wide enough to push his eyebrows toward his hairline and show his teeth.

“Very good answer.”

He leaned back. The chair creaked under him. A wet ring remained where the glass had been.

“One more question. This is important.” He leaned forward again. “Do you believe that a person can be taught integrity, or is it something they either have or they don’t?”

They must know what they’re doing, Şafak thought.

“I think integrity can be practiced,” he said. “It’s like a muscle. Some people are born with more of it, but everyone can develop it if the conditions are right.”

The man closed the folder for the last time. He stood up. He extended his hand.

“Welcome aboard.”

Şafak walked out of the building into the afternoon sun, lighter than he’d been in months. The receptionist smiled at him on the way out. She already knew. The woman in the stiff blazer was still in the waiting room. The young man with the short tie was gone.

Outside, he called his mother. She cried. He called Hakan. Hakan said finally. He walked to the Starbucks on the corner and ordered a Venti Java Chip Frappuccino, which cost more than his dinner the night before.

The email arrived two hours later. He opened it on the bus home, standing, holding the overhead rail with one hand and his phone with the other, reading between the jolts.

Dear Şafak,

We are delighted to confirm your appointment as Junior Policy Analyst at the Strategic Planning Division of…

He read the sentence again. Then once more.

He had never applied to be a policy analyst, never heard of the Strategic Planning Division. Someone had sent him to the wrong office.

He could call. He could say there’s been a mistake, I’m not who you think I am, I was here for the marketing position on the third floor.

The bus stopped. Someone got off. Someone got on. The doors closed, and they moved again.

Şafak put his phone in his pocket.

He started on Monday.
