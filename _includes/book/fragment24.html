Handled

A silver laptop and a manila folder rested on the mahogany desk. He opened the laptop and began to read.

When the desk phone rang, he kept reading. Through the second ring, his fingers hit the keys, and on the third, he pressed send. Only when it rang a fourth time did he raise his head and lift the receiver.

"Speaking."

A hurried voice spilled from the earpiece. He let it run.

"Step by step," he said. "Do not confuse me."

He listened until the voice slowed.

"Alright," he said. "We will talk about this."

He placed the receiver back in its cradle.

The door opened and his secretary stepped in, carrying a black leather planner. She did not knock. It was precisely four o'clock.

She stopped at the edge of the desk.

"The dinner reservation is confirmed," she said. "For your wife's birthday."

He looked up from the screen, first at the open planner in her hands, then at her face.

"Yes."

She held his gaze for a second before closing the planner.

"Thank you," he said.

She nodded and left.

Once the door closed behind her, he pressed his palms flat against the mahogany, then shut the laptop.

Inside the car, the traffic was barely audible.

"The margin is too tight," his friend said from the passenger seat, loosening his tie. "If the quarter ends low, they will pull the funding."

"They will not," he said. He watched the road, his hands resting at ten and two on the leather steering wheel. "The projections are stable."

His friend shifted, sliding down in the seat and lifting his left leg to rest the side of his shoe against the lower edge of the dashboard.

"Still," his friend said. "It is a risk we don't have to take right now."

"It is a calculated shift," he said.

He lifted his hand from the wheel, extended his index finger, and tapped it once against the side of his friend's knee.

His friend dropped his foot back to the floor mat and sat up.

"What did you get her?"

"It's handled."

"Calculated," his friend repeated. "But they want guarantees."

He returned his hand to the wheel.

"I will give them guarantees," he said.

"Legacy lane in four hundred meters. Manual control will engage."

"You're taking the long way as usual," his friend said.

Şafak did not answer.

His hands tightened on the wheel.

His suit jacket hung over the back of his chair. He wore a white shirt.

As he lowered his wine glass, a single drop of dark red liquid detached from the rim and landed on his chest. The red seeped into the white cotton, expanding in a perfect, dark circle.

His wife stopped eating. Without speaking, she looked at the red mark on his chest, then up at his face, and waited.

He placed the glass on the table.

"Excuse me," he said.

He stood up. As he walked across the dining room, a waiter stepped aside for him. Their eyes met for a second, and the waiter inclined his head. Şafak continued toward the restrooms.

Four minutes passed before he returned to the table and sat down.

His wife looked at his chest. There was no stain, and no damp patch from scrubbing. A single faint line ran down the center, from collar to waist.

He picked up his fork.

"The flight is booked for Thursday," he said.

She picked up her glass, and they continued talking.

· · ·

He unlocked the front door. Laughter drifted from the living room as he walked down the hall. Four friends were on the sofas, holding drinks. He stopped at the threshold.

"Good evening," he said, and nodded.

Without waiting for the conversation to resume, he turned and walked into the kitchen.

His wife stood at the stove. He took off his suit jacket, hung it on the back of a wooden chair, and rolled his white sleeves up to his elbows. After washing and drying his hands at the sink, he stepped up to the counter beside her.

She hummed a low tune, tossing the vegetables in the pan with a flick of her wrist before turning smoothly to pull a stack of plates from the overhead cabinet.

He picked up a discarded garlic peel from the marble and dropped it into the bin, then took a damp cloth to wipe an invisible residue from the counter.

She reached across him for the olive oil, poured a thin stream into the pan, and tasted the sauce from the edge of a wooden spoon. "Almost there," she murmured to the stove.

He turned the burner dial down by a fraction of a millimeter, took the olive oil bottle from where she had left it, and placed it back in the pantry.

One of the men walked into the kitchen with a glass of red wine and leaned against the island.

"Need any help in here?" the man asked.

She smiled over her shoulder. "No, we have it under control. Go sit."

The man chuckled, set his wine glass down on the marble island, and began talking about the traffic on the bridge, gesturing with his free hand.

Şafak stood still and listened, his eyes on the base of the wine glass resting directly on the marble surface.

"Coming," the man called out in response to a voice from the living room. He turned and walked out, leaving the half-full glass behind.

Şafak stepped forward, pinched the stem of the glass, and moved it ten centimeters to the right, placing it dead center on a square slate coaster.

"Plates," she said.

She spooned the food in fluid, generous arcs. He took the plates two at a time and carried them into the dining room, setting them down at each place setting as the guests moved to the table and chairs scraped against the floor.

Before he pulled his own chair out, he stopped and looked back through the open doorway. The stove was off, the counters were wiped clean, and the olive oil was put away.

He sat down and unfolded his linen napkin across his lap.

"The quarterly reports are due on Monday," he said.

She laughed at something a guest whispered, and passed the salad bowl down the table.

· · ·

It was morning, and the hallway was dim. He stood at the front door, briefcase in his right hand, keys in his left.

He turned to face the living room. The sofa cushions were straight and the throw blanket was folded. The coffee table was clear.

A single glass stood on the side table by the armchair, half full of what looked like water. It caught the light once as he turned.

He set the briefcase and keys down, walked to the side table, and picked up the glass. The watch moved on his wrist and he let it. In the kitchen, he poured the water into the sink, rinsed the glass, and placed it upside down on the drying rack.

The house was silent.

He stood at the sink, looking at the clean glass, then reached out and tightened the faucet handle, though no water was dripping.
