The Joy

The last day of school smelled like warm asphalt and cheap erasers. June had already started doing its work on the city, the tar going soft under sandals, the balconies hung with laundry. It was the early nineties.

The report card was perfect. Pekiyi across the board, every line. Şafak held the thin card in both hands, careful not to crease it, and walked out of the school gates into the afternoon. His father was waiting by the car, a boxy sedan with vinyl seats that branded your thighs if you sat down too fast. He glanced at the card and nodded.

“Haydi, kasetçiye gidelim.”

The shop was squeezed between a bakkal and a tailor, its walls lined floor to ceiling with VHS cases, their plastic spines faded from the sun. The cases were empty. Display copies, lighter than they should have been. The tapes lived behind the counter, and if someone else had rented the one you wanted, you could only stare at the cover and wait. Sometimes for weeks.

At eye level: Superman IV.

Christopher Reeve, cape billowing, fist raised. He brought the case to the counter with both hands. The owner checked behind him. The tape was there.

His father talked to the owner for a while. Then they walked out with it.

Outside, his legs moved on their own. He hopped off the curb and swung his arms and looked up at the sky and worked out the exact trajectory he would take if he could fly. All he needed was the cape. He made a note to negotiate with his mother about a towel.

Report card: perfect. Superman: in the bag.

· · ·

They did not go straight home.

His father pulled over on a side street outside an apartment building with a rusty garden gate and a vine-covered balcony. Five minutes, he said. He wanted to see a friend.

The friend was a large man with a mustache, in a living room that smelled of cigarette smoke and strong tea. Tulip glasses stood on a brass tray. The television was on. The two men fell into it instantly, reunited after years.

Şafak sat on the edge of a chair that was too big for him, the tape on his lap, the report card folded in his back pocket, and waited.

Then the man turned to him. He leaned in, reached over, and gave his cheek one of those pinches. He squeezed and smiled.

“Yakışıklı, senin nasıl gidiyor bakalım? Kaç manitan var?”

He was seven. He knew about Superman. He did not know what a manita was.

The word went around in his head. Manita. Manita. It sounded like something that belonged on a report card.

And the man had asked how many.

He straightened up in the chair.

“Hiç yok amca. Hepsi pekiyi.”

The silence lasted three seconds. On the fourth, the room exploded.

His father threw his head back and laughed, mouth open, shoulders shaking. Şafak had never heard him laugh like that. He was a quiet man. He smiled plenty. The friend grabbed his own knee and the tea nearly went with it. The laughter fed itself, passing from one man to the other until neither could stop.

Şafak smiled along. He had said something brilliant, clearly. People laughed. He did not mind. He had things to do, and the thing to do was get home and press play.

· · ·

That evening, after the cape had gone back to being a towel and the Fanta had gone flat in its bottle, his father called him into the kitchen. He had a small box in his hand.

“Karne hediyen,” he said.

A silver watch. Analog. Too big for his wrist.

“Evladiyelik,” he said, and helped him with the clasp.

He wore it the rest of the summer.
