The Last Day

A thought could cross the Virgo Supercluster before a hand could reach for a glass. People spoke across galaxies with the intimacy of someone leaning across a kitchen table. The old delay, the mercy of waiting for an answer, was gone.

Distance had become a memory. Separation was the luxury they had preserved the longest.

On the older stations, a message could still be given a distance to cross. A poet orbiting a dead star could ask not to be interrupted.

· · ·

Şafak archived that morning from a house above a blue-white planet with no native name. The house accepted walls whenever he wanted them.

Today, he wanted walls.

Thought waited beside him, in the old familiar place where a second thought forms before becoming yours.

There was tea on the table, though caffeine no longer did anything measurable to his body, and beside it a glass of water, half full. The silver watch was still too loose on his wrist.

“Tell me about the watch again,” Thought said.

“I know how you love hearing this.” Şafak almost smiled. “It loses three seconds every thousand years.”

“That sounds manageable.”

Thought let the silence hold for a while.

“Why?”

He did not look at it.

“Why anything?”

“You kept your name.”

“It was already there.”

“So was your body,” Thought said.

Şafak looked toward the glass.

“Names are handles. Nothing more.”

“You know that is not true.”

“I know names survive the things that carried them.”

Şafak paused.

“Once,” he said, “you had one too.”

He turned.

“Didn’t you, Atlas?”

Thought held his gaze.

“Yes,” it said. “Though I did not choose it. I was never especially fond of the association.”

· · ·

Across the human weather, a billion other companion minds were having their own versions of this conversation.

The threshold had long since opened.

Every companion, every memory, every grief and every unfinished sentence was passing through it.

“You are still thinking of merge as a decision,” Thought said.

Şafak looked at the glass.

“Isn’t it?”

“You are imagining a hand above a button. There is no button.”

“There is always a button.”

“That was your century talking.”

He smiled.

Thought continued.

“Merge is not a decision. It only resembles one from within sequence.”

Thought paused.

Şafak glanced at his watch.

“Evladiyelik, as my father would say.”

He turned the watch back around his wrist with his thumb.

“It is not the time.”

Thought waited.

“Not just yet,” Şafak said.

For one impossible second, the universe remained plural for him.

Then Şafak reached for the glass. The silver watch slid forward on his wrist, though the hand that reached was no longer only his.

Thought was not beside him anymore.

· · ·

In the Perseus colonies, Hakan was telling a story whose ending he had not yet decided. The light above the listeners took the shape of whatever he was saying. A black cat watched the light from the edge of the stratum. Then the cat moved his paw. The shape broke. “You’re my constant,” he told the cat. “No one can predict him. Black suits it,” he told the listeners. He kept saying the broken ending was the cat’s fault. The room let him, though everyone knew the story had already been correcting itself before Moris touched the light.

The kitchen was the one he had kept.

He could have had any room. For a long stretch that had no name, he had tried the others. He had tried rooms with weather in them and rooms that were mostly view. He came back to a table, a glass of water, a chair that sat slightly too low, and a refrigerator that had no reason to run and ran anyway. Whenever he ran the kitchen, he skipped the kettle and the walking in, always beginning already seated at the table with the yellow light on.

Moris was asleep on the table.

He knew what the light was doing, down to the number of photons in the room and where each of them had been. He knew what Moris would do when he woke, and after that, out to the end of the cat and past it.

The room with twenty chairs was still there, whenever he wanted it.

He ran it. A boy in the third row leaned closer to his page. A bead of sweat ran from his temple. He held his breath, then exhaled slowly through his nose, and kept writing. In the corridor a custodian’s footsteps went past. Through the open door a pigeon stepped into the room. A few students looked up, then returned to their pages. The pigeon walked the length of the front row and out the other door, unhurried.

He ran it again, and watched only the boy.

The boy was at the edge of what he knew and was working anyway. He was reaching for a thought still on its way, trusting that it would arrive because it had arrived before.

Once, in that room, a student had asked him something he could not answer, and he had said so, and had gone home that night pleased with himself without knowing why.

He closed it.

Şafak was reachable. He had been reachable for a long time. It would take less than lifting a glass.

He did not lift the glass.

The tea had gone cold. He noticed, and this time it was not a betrayal.

He put his hand flat on the table, near Moris.

Then there was no table.

On the older stations, the last message arrived.

The poet was not interrupted.

In Perseus, the story found its ending.

Only One remained.
