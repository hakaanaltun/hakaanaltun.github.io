The Road

The car asked if we wanted music.

Neither of us answered right away. Şafak had his temple against the glass, watching the spaces between the trees. I was watching the road. The car didn’t need me to watch anything. But my hands still floated near the wheel.

“Something calm,” Şafak said.

The car played something acoustic. Spanish guitar, maybe Portuguese. It had learned us well enough by now—no lyrics when the mood was ambiguous, nothing percussive before noon.

We were heading south toward a cove Şafak had found. He said it was past Kuşadası, tucked beneath a cliff that faced west. We were going for two days. Nothing was planned. We hadn’t spoken in seven months.

Şafak turned from the window. “Who’s got Moris?”

“A friend down the street. She goes to see him every day. I asked her not to leave without playing with him.”

My phone buzzed before we’d cleared the city limits. Two notifications were stacked on the screen. The first: Lena has submitted a request—Grocery order (14 items). Review & merge? The second appeared below it like a footnote: Your sleep quality last night was 43% below baseline. Lena recommends postponing essential decisions today.

I dismissed the second one and opened the first. Lena—my assistant, though calling her that was like calling your lungs your breathing assistant—had drafted the weekly order. Mostly the usual: eggs, bread, that one brand of olives I liked but could never remember the name of. She’d swapped the regular milk for lactose-free. An annotation floated beneath the change: Based on your last three conversations about bloating. I approved it with a tap. Merged.

Şafak’s phone buzzed a second later. He looked at it, frowned, and put it back down.

“What?” I said.

“Atlas wants me to confirm the accommodation. I told it we’d figure it out when we got there.”

“So reject it.”

“I did. Twice. It keeps opening new requests.”

“Atlas sends an agent for each one,” I said. “You’ve been turning away the same intern all morning. That’s because you haven’t told it what you actually want.”

“I want it to wait.”

“It doesn’t know what waiting looks like if you haven’t defined the condition.”

He exhaled through his nose.

“Just tell Atlas to queue it until we’re thirty minutes out,” I said.

He picked up his phone. I heard him murmur something—the private tone people used with their assistants. A few seconds later, his phone went quiet.

I’d told Lena to wait a few weeks earlier. I was looking through a revised syllabus she’d drafted. Every assessment aligned too neatly with the rubric, and I’d typed: Don’t send it yet. Just wait. Lena had asked: Waiting for what? And I’d answered: I’ll know when I see it. She’d paused, then: Understood. Queuing request.

The highway opened up past Aydın. The Aegean light flattened the landscape into an overexposed photograph. Fig trees and stone walls lined the road. An old gas station sat back from the road, its pumps gone, its canopy stripped to rusted steel. The office was now a roadside tea stop with a hand-painted sign and a few plastic chairs under a sagging awning. The car pulled into a charging bay beside the office without asking. A notification: Charging session initiated. Estimated duration: 8 minutes. Payment merged.

No one got out.

We passed a truck carrying three white wind turbine blades on a flatbed that looked too small for them. The truck was in the legacy lane. An old man in a cap was driving, eyes on the road, his hands on the wheel. The car moved left, giving the truck a wide berth. I watched them shrink in the side mirror, like bones being carried away.

Şafak was talking about work. His team was designing a long-range strategy while Atlas ran the financial simulations and managed the regulatory filings. Şafak went with what felt right to him. His assistant handled what was right. When they disagreed, he would submit a revision, and Atlas would push back with a counterproposal backed by data and precedent.

“Sometimes it feels like I’m arguing with a very polite version of my father,” he said.

I laughed. “At least Atlas gives reasons.”

“That’s what makes it worse. You can’t storm out of a conversation with someone who responds in bullet points and cites compliance frameworks from 2023.”

He said “reject” instead of “refuse.” He said “I’ll draft something” instead of “I’ll think about it.” We all did.

Around noon the car suggested we stop for lunch. A notification appeared on both our phones simultaneously: Joint lunch recommendation drafted by Lena & Atlas. Coastal restaurant, 4 km ahead. Menu pre-filtered for your dietary preferences. Review & merge?

Şafak stared at his screen. “They made us a joint draft.”

“They coordinate.”

“I don’t want a joint draft. I want to decide what I eat when I see the menu.”

“There aren’t really menus anymore.”

“I know there aren’t really menus anymore.”

He rejected the draft. I watched him do it—his thumb pressing down on the screen with more force than the gesture required.

We didn’t stop for lunch.
