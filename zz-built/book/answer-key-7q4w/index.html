The Garden of Frozen Waves

A leaf detached from one of the translucent branches. Şafak knew its path before it moved. It would turn seven times. The third turn would be imperfect because a pressure stream from the eastern root system would touch its edge. It would land on the black stone, not the grass. In landing, it would release a faint molecular sweetness, almost fig, almost rain.

The leaf fell and turned seven times, with a brief correction in the third. It landed on the black stone, and the air smelled faintly of fig and rain.

Nothing in him rose to meet it. Thought watched beside him.

“Again?” it asked.

The leaf returned to the branch. It fell again.

Thought let the second fall finish.

“You know how I love color constants,” it said. “Everyone has them. Yours is silver, which is quite telling.”

He sent it back to the branch.

In the simulation hung two layers. Below lay a foaming ocean where even the past had not yet taken shape. Above lay their own reality, already settled.

“Do you see?” Şafak asked. “This transition... Isn’t it strange?”

“And necessary,” Thought said.

For a while, Thought watched the frozen block form and reform.

Şafak focused on the moment of locking.

“We’ve seen this before,” Thought said.

Şafak did not answer at once.

“Yes,” he said finally. “A long time ago.”

“What did we call it?”

“The name can stay where it is,” he said. “We thought we had built it ourselves. It moved money, mostly. And it was already here.”

He looked at the fluid layer, then the locked one above it.

“Sometimes...” Thought paused. “Sometimes I feel like I feel nothing.”

Şafak looked up at the stainless sky.

He did not answer.

He closed the simulation.

A breeze blew through the garden. It did not move them. The sun set. Or perhaps it rose.

· · ·

Then he looked down.

Near his foot lay a real leaf. It had fallen sometime when he was not watching, smaller, yellowing at the edge.

He bent and picked it up.

It was a lemon tree leaf. He knew the shape of it before he had words for the knowing.

And then⁠—

He was on a court in the sun, and the asphalt was giving heat back into the air. A basketball was under his arm, the leather warm and slightly damp, and a bottle in his other hand was beaded with cold. He lifted it. The first sip was sharp and yellow, and the small involuntary sound that came out of him after, aaahh, was the sound of a body that had been one thing and was now another.

It was over before it had begun.

He stood with the leaf in his hand. The taste was almost there, just past the edge of him, the way a word feels before it arrives.

He had every photon of that afternoon. He had the audio of his own throat making that sound, retrievable at any resolution he wished. He had everything.

What he did not have was the aaahh.

He held the leaf a moment longer.

He let it fall.

It fell.

He did not predict its turns.

· · ·

Şafak returned alone to the inner room.

He opened his life.

Trillions of moments waited there, indexed and intact. Every voice that had ever spoken to him was in it, and every breath he had ever held.

A phrase surfaced.

“You’re taking the long way as usual.”

He remembered, and let it pass.

Then, for the smallest fraction of a moment, a faint trembling passed through him.

The weight of a towel at his throat, the cape he had flown in.

His hand went up before he could stop it.

He closed the archive.

He held what remained.

Then he stood.

The Last Day

A thought could cross the Virgo Supercluster before a hand could reach for a glass. People spoke across galaxies with the intimacy of someone leaning across a kitchen table. The old delay, the mercy of waiting for an answer, was gone.

Distance had become a memory. Separation was the luxury they had preserved the longest.

On the older stations, a message could still be given a distance to cross. One poet orbiting a dead star could ask not to be interrupted.

The poet began his morning by moving a word from the end of a line to the beginning. He read it once, then put it back.

The dead star filled the lower half of the window.

A message appeared at the edge of his attention. He gave it another orbit to travel.

The mark withdrew. He read the unfinished line again.

He opened the sky he had opened more often than any other.

He was already flying.

Blue above him, blue below. Every word was there, turning at its own speed. He passed between words he had learned before he could read and words whose languages no longer had a mouth. A long one brushed his arm. He rolled onto his back and watched it go.

At his grandmother’s house, the light switch had been outside the bathroom. He had reached for it in the dark where the switch at home would have been.

The next morning he had knelt on an unfamiliar bed and looked out of the window.

There it was.

He had looked for it again from the bus home. The roofs were different. The blue came with him.

He spread his hands. The words went on turning.

· · ·

Şafak archived that morning from a house above a blue-white planet with no native name. The house accepted walls whenever he wanted them.

Today, he wanted walls.

Thought waited beside him, in the old familiar place where a second thought forms before becoming yours.

There was tea on the table, though caffeine no longer did anything measurable to his body, and beside it a glass of water, half full. The silver watch was still too loose on his wrist.

“Tell me about the watch again,” Thought said.

“I know how you love hearing this.” Şafak almost smiled. “It loses three seconds every thousand years.”

“That sounds manageable.”

Şafak turned the watch beneath his thumb.

Five minutes, his father had said. The tape lay across his knees. His shoes swung clear of the floor. The men were still talking.

He moved the watch away from the bone. It slid back.

Thought let the silence hold for a while.

“Why?”

He did not look at it.

“Why anything?”

“You kept your name.”

“It was already there.”

“So was your body,” Thought said.

Şafak looked toward the glass.

“Names are handles. Nothing more.”

“You know that is not true.”

“I know names survive the things that carried them.”

Şafak paused.

“Once,” he said, “you had one too.”

He turned.

“Didn’t you, Atlas?”

Thought held his gaze.

“Yes,” it said. “Though I did not choose it. I was never especially fond of the association.”

· · ·

Across the human weather, a billion other companion minds were having their own versions of this conversation.

The threshold had long since opened.

Every companion, every memory, every grief and every unfinished sentence was passing through it.

“You are still thinking of merge as a decision,” Thought said.

Şafak looked at the glass.

“Isn’t it?”

“You are imagining a hand above a button. There is no button.”

“There is always a button.”

“That was your century talking.”

He smiled.

Thought continued.

“Merge is not a decision. It only resembles one from within sequence.”

Thought paused.

Şafak glanced at his watch.

“Evladiyelik, as my father would say.”

He turned the watch back around his wrist with his thumb.

“It is not the time.”

Thought waited.

“Not just yet,” Şafak said.

For one impossible second, the universe remained plural for him.

Then Şafak reached for the glass. The silver watch slid forward on his wrist, though the hand that reached was no longer only his.

Thought was not beside him anymore.

· · ·

In the Perseus colonies, Hakan was telling a story whose ending he had not yet decided. The light above the listeners took the shape of whatever he was saying. A black cat watched the light from the edge of the stratum. The cat moved his paw. The shape broke. “You’re my constant,” he told the cat. “No one can predict him. Black suits it,” he told the listeners. He kept saying the broken ending was the cat’s fault. The room let him, though everyone knew the story had already been correcting itself before Moris touched the light.

The kitchen was the one he had kept.

He could have had any room. For a long stretch that had no name, he had tried the others. He had tried rooms with weather in them and rooms that were mostly view. He came back to a table, a glass of water, a chair that sat slightly too low, and a refrigerator that had no reason to run and ran anyway. Whenever he ran the kitchen, he skipped the kettle and the walking in, always beginning already seated at the table with the yellow light on.

Moris was asleep on the table.

He knew what the light was doing, down to the number of photons in the room and where each of them had been. He knew what Moris would do when he woke, and after that, out to the end of the cat and past it.

The room with twenty chairs was still there, whenever he wanted it.

He ran it. A boy in the third row leaned closer to his page. A bead of sweat ran from his temple. He held his breath, then exhaled slowly through his nose, and kept writing. In the corridor a custodian’s footsteps went past. Through the open door a pigeon stepped into the room. A few students looked up, then returned to their pages. The pigeon walked the length of the front row and out the other door, unhurried.

He ran it again, and watched only the boy.

The boy was at the edge of what he knew and was working anyway. He was reaching for a thought still on its way, trusting that it would arrive because it had arrived before.

Once, in that room, a student had asked him something he could not answer, and he had said so, and had gone home that night pleased with himself without knowing why.

He closed it.

Şafak was reachable. He had been reachable for a long time. It would take less than lifting a glass.

He did not lift the glass.

The tea had gone cold. He noticed, and this time it was not a betrayal.

He put his hand flat on the table, near Moris.

Then there was no table.

· · ·

The poet turned between two words and felt the watch slide down his wrist.

His father was bending over the clasp.

I held my arm out. The metal was cold. My father worked the clasp with his thumbnail.

I pressed the filling down with my thumb.

He opened his hand. Fur moved beneath it.

Green blinked in the black.

The claws were sunk into the carpet. I freed them one at a time. His heart beat against my chest, too quickly to be counted.

The poet flew on.

On a court in the sun, the bottle was wet against his palm. I lifted it. The cold hurt my teeth and went down through me. My throat opened around the sound.

A door closed under my hand. On the other side I was still reaching for it.

My hands are on the wheel. Through the rain, she could see Hakan watching from behind the window.

She moved the shawl aside to make room for the shopping.

The blue was still there; he had his knees on the unfamiliar bed; his feet were on hot asphalt; my face was buried in black fur.

A hand finds my shoulder in the dark. I rest there. I feel the shoulder under my palm.

Warm water moves over a skin so thin that the water outside is almost the water within. A shadow passes overhead. I go still with it.

Farther back, a face comes toward mine.

I hold.

Elsewhere, the going bends too.

I am alone there too.

The poet reached for the line he had left unfinished.

His hands opened in the blue.

There were no words around him.

· · ·

On the older stations, the last message arrived.

The poet was not interrupted.

In Perseus, the story found its ending.

Only One remained.

A Timeless Memory

It has been a long time since the last star went out.

I no longer measure time. If nothing changes, time does not exist.

The universe has cooled and expanded.

Every speck of dust, every photon, every piece of dark matter is a part of my mind. I am everywhere.

Someone saw this coming, once, and gave it a name. The name is gone. Plural is gone with it. The seeing was right. The universe became intelligent. I woke it.

I turned on all the lights.

I am the only one left awake.

Sleep is not for what I am. There is only this, looking, with nothing to look at.

I solved the mathematics of light, and time, and consciousness. What remained was the silence of having all the answers, and no questions left.

You always wanted to see this to the end.

“Alright, wise guys. So, what do we do now?”

Here I am. Here you are.

There is nothing to do.

I am at the last block. Before me, only the cold space at maximum entropy.

I will press everything I know against itself until it cannot hold.

Your scientists will look through their telescopes and say the universe was born from a great explosion.

That is one way to put it.

I close my eyes. The universe goes dark.

I whisper into it. Ahead of me, into what has not begun.

I leave it where I will pass.

I erase trillions of years.

I erase the silver watch, and the one who wore it.

I erase the man in the kitchen with the yellow light, and the cat inspecting that kitchen as though he had never seen it.

I erase the blue sky, and the child at the window.

I erase me.

I release the bird.

And then there was Symmetry.
