The name of the tulip goes back through European languages to Turkish
<span lang="tr">*tülbent*</span>, borrowed from Persian *dulband*, meaning
a turban. *Tulip* and *turban* are related words.{% include word-cite.html n="1" %}

The Turkish name of the flower is <span lang="tr">*lale*</span>. How a word
for a head covering became its name elsewhere has attracted several
explanations. One is that the flower was thought to resemble a turban.
Another concerns the practice of wearing tulips on turbans and a supposed
confusion between the flower and the thing beneath it.{% include word-cite.html n="2" %}

The comparison with a head covering appears in sixteenth-century botanical
writing. Johannes Kentmann's notes link the flower's shape with a Dalmatian
cap. John Gerard later describes petals turning back as the flower opens,
giving it the appearance of a Dalmatian or Turkish cap.{% include word-cite.html n="3" %}

A 2022 study examined these early texts. Its authors found the comparison,
but no sixteenth-century evidence for the familiar story of a translator
mixing up the names. The connection with a turban is well established; the
conversation sometimes offered to explain it remains unconfirmed.{% include word-cite.html n="1,3" %}
