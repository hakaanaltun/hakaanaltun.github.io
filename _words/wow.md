*Wow* can sound very modern. The Dictionaries of the Scots Language records
it in *Peblis to the Play*, with the example dated before 1500, and in Gavin
Douglas's writing of 1513. People were putting this familiar reaction into
words more than five centuries ago.{% include word-cite.html n="1" %}

Those early examples carry a range of feelings. The dictionary identifies
astonishment and fear as well as admiration. In one example from 1540,
someone cries out at what they see. In another, a frightened speaker asks
for room to get away. The sound could accompany an alarming sight as readily
as an impressive one.{% include word-cite.html n="1" %}

The word had renewed popularity in the early twentieth century and again
during the 1960s. It also became a verb. By 1924, American slang used *wow*
for overwhelming someone with delight or amazement, the sense we use when
a performer wows an audience.{% include word-cite.html n="2" %}

In 1977, the exclamation acquired a place in astronomy. On August 15,
Ohio State University's Big Ear radio telescope detected an unusually
strong radio signal during a search for extraterrestrial intelligence.
A few days later, the volunteer astronomer Jerry Ehman was reviewing the
computer printout when he noticed the sequence *6EQUJ5*. He marked the
characters in red and wrote "Wow!" in the margin. His colleague John Kraus
began calling it the Wow! signal.{% include word-cite.html n="3" %}

The six characters recorded changes in the signal's strength. They showed
it growing stronger and then fading as the Earth's rotation carried the
telescope's view past that part of the sky. The pattern caught Ehman's
attention because it matched what he would expect from a compact radio
source in the sky.{% include word-cite.html n="3,4" %}

Follow-up observations have not detected the same signal again, and its
origin remains unresolved. The name came from Ehman's handwritten
reaction as he examined the data.{% include word-cite.html n="3,4" %}

*Wow, I hadn't realized how high we had climbed.*
