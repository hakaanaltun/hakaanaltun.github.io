Silly began with good fortune. Its Old English ancestor meant happy or
fortunate, and the related German word <span lang="de">*selig*</span>
still means blessed or blissful. In Middle English, forms such as *sely*
and *seely* carried meanings including blessed and innocent.
{% include word-cite.html n="1,2" %}

Innocence could also suggest being harmless or unable to defend oneself.
The word came to describe someone deserving pity, then someone weak. By
the sixteenth century it could describe a person judged to lack sense.
These meanings developed along overlapping paths, with innocence and
helplessness giving way to foolishness.{% include word-cite.html n="1" %}

Today silly can be a mild criticism of someone’s judgement or an affectionate
word for playful behaviour. The older meanings survive in texts where a
“silly” person may be helpless or humble.{% include word-cite.html n="2" %}
