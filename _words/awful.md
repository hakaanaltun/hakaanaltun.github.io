Awful carries *awe* inside it. In its older uses, something awful inspired
fear or reverence. The word could describe the power of something that
made people feel small or deeply respectful.{% include word-cite.html n="1" %}

That strength gradually weakened in everyday use. By around the beginning
of the nineteenth century, awful was being used for things that were very
bad or disagreeable. It also became an intensifier: an *awful lot* is a
great quantity. A word suited to dread could now describe a disappointing
meal.{% include word-cite.html n="1,2" %}

The older meaning remains available, especially in literary writing.
Merriam-Webster gives George Eliot’s phrase “awful loveliness” as an
example. There the beauty inspires awe. Reading it as a complaint would
change the sense of the passage.{% include word-cite.html n="2" %}

*The weather was awful, so we stayed inside.*
