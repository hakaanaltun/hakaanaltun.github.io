Bankruptcy is what happens when someone cannot pay what they owe. It is built
on *bankrupt*, which came into English from the Italian
<span lang="it">*banca rotta*</span>: a broken
bench.{% include word-cite.html n="1,2" %}

The bench is real. <span lang="it">*Banca*</span> was the ordinary Italian word
for the table a money dealer worked at, and it is the same word that gave us
*bank*. <span lang="it">*Rotta*</span> is broken, from the Latin
<span lang="la">*rumpere*</span>.{% include word-cite.html n="2" %}

The breaking is the story. A dealer who could not meet his debts had his table
broken in front of him, and that was the end of his trade. Samuel Johnson retold
it in his dictionary of 1755.{% include word-cite.html n="2,3" %}

Nobody has produced evidence that it happened. The reading the dictionaries
settle on is that <span lang="it">*rotta*</span> is figurative here, in the way
an English business is said to be broken, and that no carpentry was involved. I
could not confirm the custom.{% include word-cite.html n="1,2" %}

Breaking the instrument of an office was a real way of ending one. A degraded
knight had his belt cut, his spurs struck off and his sword broken over his
head; a Garter knight's banner was pulled down and thrown out of the chapel;
and judges have snapped the nib of the pen that signed a death sentence, on the
reasoning that it should not be used again.{% include word-cite.html n="4,5" %}

Nothing I found joins any of that to the money dealer's table. It does explain
why the table is easy to picture: a reader who knows that a broken sword ends a
knighthood will read a broken bench the same way.

*The company filed for bankruptcy.*
