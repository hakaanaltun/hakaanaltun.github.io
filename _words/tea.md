English *tea* and Turkish <span lang="tr">*çay*</span> belong to the two
largest families of names for the same drink. Both go back to Chinese.
Most Chinese varieties use a form like *cha*; Southern Min, spoken in
Fujian and Taiwan, has forms like *te*. Which one a language borrowed
often tells us who brought the tea.{% include word-cite.html n="1" %}

Dutch traders bought through Xiamen, then known in Europe as Amoy, and
took *te* into Dutch as *thee*. English *tea* and French *thé* belong to
that family. Overland trade carried forms of *cha* across much of Asia
and into eastern Europe. “Tea by sea, cha by land” describes a broad
pattern, with exceptions. Portuguese traders also arrived by sea, but
their route went through Macao, where they encountered Cantonese *cha*.
Portuguese still calls the drink *chá*.{% include word-cite.html n="1" %}

A Portuguese tea drinker helped make it fashionable at the English
court. Catherine of Braganza married Charles II in 1662 and brought her
tea-drinking habits with her. London coffee houses were already serving
it in the 1650s. The East India Company placed its first order for tea
in 1664, as demand grew.{% include word-cite.html n="2" %}

By the middle of the nineteenth century, tea was drunk daily across
British social classes. Afternoon tea gave it a meal of its own between
lunch and dinner. The sugar in the cup had a history too: its growing
availability depended on enslaved labour on Caribbean plantations.
The domestic habit was tied to the commerce and coercion of
empire.{% include word-cite.html n="3" %}

Across the Atlantic, the East India Company’s tea became the object of
a protest. The Tea Act of 1773 helped the financially troubled company
sell directly to the American colonies. It kept the existing tea duty
and gave the company advantages over colonial merchants; it imposed no
new tax. Opponents feared both its monopoly and the precedent of
accepting taxation by a Parliament in which they had no
representatives.{% include word-cite.html n="4" %} On 16 December 1773,
protesters boarded three ships in Boston and threw their tea into the
harbour. The event became known as the Boston Tea
Party.{% include word-cite.html n="5" %}

In Japan, the *cha* sound is part of <span lang="ja">*chadō*</span>,
the way of tea. The practice developed over centuries, with the
sixteenth-century tea master Sen no Rikyū central to the tradition
followed by Urasenke.{% include word-cite.html n="6" %} A host chooses
utensils, a hanging scroll and sweets with the season and the guests in
mind. Flowers may be those growing at that time of year. The arrangement
should help guests feel cool in summer and warm in winter. Learning to
receive the tea and appreciate what has been prepared is part of the
practice too.{% include word-cite.html n="7" %}

In Türkiye, tea was known well before the Republic. Seyyid Mehmed İzzet’s
*Çay Risalesi*, published in 1878, discussed its varieties and how to
prepare it.{% include word-cite.html n="8" %} Republican policy helped
establish domestic production: a 1924 law supported tea cultivation
around Rize, and the first factory opened in 1947. By 1963, domestic
production could meet the country’s demand.{% include word-cite.html n="9" %}

Coffee had already left its name in the morning meal. Turkish
<span lang="tr">*kahvaltı*</span>, breakfast, comes from
<span lang="tr">*kahve altı*</span>, literally “under coffee”: food eaten
before drinking coffee. Tea now accompanies that breakfast, often in
a tulip-shaped glass.{% include word-cite.html n="10" %}
