The word *biscuit* once described how the food was made. Early French
*pain bescuit* meant bread cooked twice. A second baking dried it out,
helping it keep on a long journey. The phrase was shortened, and English
borrowed the name.{% include word-cite.html n="1" %}

Ship's biscuits became a staple of life at sea. They could be so hard that
biting into them was difficult, and stored supplies were vulnerable to
insects. Royal Museums Greenwich shows a surviving biscuit with holes made
by biscuit beetles.{% include word-cite.html n="2" %} Its collection also
includes a biscuit made to an 1846 recipe.{% include word-cite.html n="3" %}

The word eventually covered foods made without a second baking. In Britain,
a biscuit is usually what Americans call a cookie. In the United States,
it commonly means a small, soft quick bread. The old cooking instruction
remains in the name of both.{% include word-cite.html n="1" %}
