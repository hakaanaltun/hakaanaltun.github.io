The metal behind cobalt blue takes its name from a goblin. German miners
used *Kobold* for a troublesome spirit, and the name became attached to
ores that frustrated their attempts to extract useful
metal.{% include word-cite.html n="1" %}

In Saxony, miners tried smelting what they believed was silver-bearing
ore. When it failed to yield the silver they expected, they blamed
goblins for bewitching it. Some cobalt ores also contained arsenic, which
could be released as poisonous fumes during processing. The material was
dangerous as well as disappointing.{% include word-cite.html n="1,2" %}

In the eighteenth century, the Swedish chemist Georg Brandt investigated
a similar ore and identified a previously unrecognized metal. The
miners' name was carried over to the element.{% include word-cite.html n="2" %}

Cobalt compounds give glass and ceramics a deep blue color. The name used
for that familiar blue preserves the miners' much less welcome experience
of the ore.{% include word-cite.html n="1" %}

*The potter used cobalt to color the glaze.*
