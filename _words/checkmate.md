Checkmate ends a game of chess: the king is attacked and cannot escape. The
word came into English in the fourteenth century from Old French
<span lang="fro">*eschec mat*</span>, from Arabic
<span lang="ar">*shāh māt*</span>.{% include word-cite.html n="1" %}

The Arabic is usually read as “the king died.” But *shāh* is the Persian
word for king, and Persian *māt* can mean astonished, so the phrase may first
have meant that the king was left helpless rather than
dead.{% include word-cite.html n="1" %}

*Check* comes from the same *shāh*, and it travelled far from the board. The
officials of the Norman kings of England counted the royal revenue with
counters on a table covered with a chequered cloth, which looked like a
chessboard, and their department became the
Exchequer.{% include word-cite.html n="2" %} The bank *cheque*, from 1798,
probably took its spelling from the
exchequer.{% include word-cite.html n="1" %}

*Three moves later it was checkmate.*
