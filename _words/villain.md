The villain in a story has a name that once belonged to a villager. The
word comes through French from Medieval Latin
<span lang="la">*villanus*</span>, a rural inhabitant or farm worker,
derived from <span lang="la">*villa*</span>.{% include word-cite.html n="1,2" %}

In medieval usage, the name carried the contempt of people who ranked
themselves above those who worked the land. A person of low birth was
assumed to have coarse manners. The judgement then extended to morals:
someone considered uncouth became someone considered wicked.
{% include word-cite.html n="1" %}

By the sixteenth century villain could mean a scoundrel. The familiar use
for a character whose evil actions drive a play or novel came later.
The spelling *villein* kept the historical sense of a peasant bound to a
lord’s land, while *villain* became the usual form of the insult.
{% include word-cite.html n="2" %}

*The villain was the most interesting character in the play.*
