A gossip was once someone connected to a family through baptism. The Old
English word *godsibb* joined God with kinship and referred to a godparent.
Its meaning widened to include a close friend or companion.{% include word-cite.html n="1,2" %}

Several meanings were still in use in Shakespeare's time. In *Henry VIII*,
the king addresses his daughter's godparents as “My noble gossips.” In
*Titus Andronicus*, Aaron calls the nurse “a long-tongued, babbling gossip.”
One use belongs to a christening ceremony. The other expresses distrust of
someone who might reveal a secret.{% include word-cite.html n="3,4" %}

The word also became associated with women attending a birth and with the
conversation at those gatherings. Its familiar sense developed alongside
older meanings that remained available to speakers and writers.{% include word-cite.html n="2" %}

*He was a terrible gossip, though everyone told him things.*
